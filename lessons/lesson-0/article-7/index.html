In this article we'll learn about Digraphs which are also called "combination kana." For those of you unfamiliar with what a Digraph is, they're basically characters that combine together two different characters to create a new sound (like "ch" in "cheese"), which is why they're often called combo characters.

In Japanese, Digraphs are created by combining characters in the いi row (きki、しshi、ちchi…) with the small variant of ゃya, ゅyu, and ょyo. When these characters are combined, the いi sound is dropped and the sounds merge together. For example, きki + ゅyu = きゅkyu. As you can see, "i" is dropped and the characters merge together to create "kyu."

33 more sounds can be created in this manner using all of the characters that we previously learned in the いi row. This is the final stretch! When you're ready, head on over to the first section below to learn your first set of Digraphs!

To type the small ゃya characters on the keyboard, all you need to do is type the romaji for the digraphs, such as kya, kyu, or kyo. However, if for some reason you want to type them alone, you can type them in romaji by prefixing the character with an "x", so xya, xyu, and xyo would type the small variations of those characters. If you're on a Kana keyboard, you can make the small characters by typing the character and then clicking the 小small key to make it small.

きゃkya きゅkyu きょkyo

First up is the きゃkya column which takes the "k" sound from きki and combines it with ゃya, ゅyu, and ょyo.


Hiragana きゃ
Romaji kya
Pronunciation kyah

きゃkya is pronounced like a combination of きki and やya, however, you do NOT pronounce the "i"; it is dropped. The sound you create should be similar to kyah ("k" sound followed by "yah"). きゃ is sometimes used as an exclamation of surprise/fear in Japanese with the あ sound extended.

A：きゃあKyah！D-Did you touch my shoulder?
B：Uh, no? Maybe it was a ghost.
A：T-There's no such thing as ghosts!!!

Hiragana きゅ
Romaji kyu
Pronunciation kyoo

きゅkyu is pronounced like a combination of きki (minus "i") and ゆyu, making a kyoo sound ("k" sound followed by "you"). It is also pronounced like the cu in "cute" and "cucumber."

Did you see the きゅcucumber that I'm growing? It's so small and きゅcute!

きゅ can even be used to write "cute" in Japanese as きゅーとkyuuto. This is typically written in Katakana, however, we haven't learned that yet. Still, who says we can't write "cute" in Hiragana? It's actually cuter written that way!

Note: The "ー" extends the "u" sound in "kyu" here. It plays the role as "う" here, making it "きゅうと."


Hiragana きょ
Romaji kyo
Pronunciation kyoh

きょkyo is pronounced like a combination of きki (minus "i") and よyo, making a kyoh sound ("k" sound followed by "yoh"). If you're familiar with Japan's former capital, Kyoto, it's pronounced exactly like that kyo!

In きょkyoto, they speak Japanese using the きょkyoto dialect.

Though we're only using one "o" here to demonstrate the sound, Kyoto is actually written as きょうとkyouto, with an extended う, in Japanese. And while we're on the topic of Kyoto, a fun fact about the Kanji for 京都きょうと is that it literally means "capital city," indicating that Kyoto was once the capital of Japan.

京capitalきょうkyou 都metropolisとto

Pronunciation gyoo

Please note that no one actually says "ぎゅう" when they hug someone in real life unless they're being silly. It's mainly an onomatopoeticimitative word used in fictional works to describe the action of hugging/squeezing, such as in manga sound effects. It is also used in online slang, similar to writing *hug* in English.

Hiragana しゃ
Romaji sha
Pronunciation shah

しゃsha is pronounced like a combination of しshi (minus the "i") and やya (minus the "y"). The sound you create should be similar to shah ("sh" sound followed by "ah"). It's also pronounced like the sha in "shaman" in American English, which is pronounced like "sh-ah-men."

I had no luck finding my escaped cat, so as a last resort I consulted a しゃshaman to divine her location.

On the topic of cats, しゃsha can also be used as an onomatopoeia for a cat's hiss.

When you frighten a cat in Japan, they go しゃあshaa!

Hiragana しゅ
Romaji shu
Pronunciation shoo

しゅshu is pronounced like a combination of しshi (minus the "i") and ゆyu (minus the "y"). The sound you create should be similar to shoo ("sh" sound followed by "oo"). It's also pronounced like "shoe" or like the shoo in "shoot."

P-Please don't しゅshoot me! I'll give you my しゅshoes!

Hiragana しょ
Romaji sho
Pronunciation shoh

しょsho is pronounced like a combination of しshi (minus the "i") and よyo (minus the "y"). The sound you create should be similar to shoh ("sh" sound followed by "oh"). It's also pronounced exactly like the word "show."

ちゃcha ちゅchu ちょcho

Next up is the ちゃcha column which takes the "ch" sound from ちchi and combines it with ゃya, ゅyu, and ょyo. However, much like the しゃsha and じゃja columns, the "y" sound is NOT pronounced here. We're essentially taking "ch" from ち and "a/u/o" from "や/ゆ/よ" to create our sounds in this column.


Hiragana ちゃ
Romaji cha
Pronunciation chah

ちゃcha is pronounced like a combination of ちchi (minus the "i") and やya (minus the "y"). The sound you create should be similar to chah ("ch" sound followed by "ah"). It's also pronounced like the cha in "gotcha" and "gacha."

A：Yo, dude! Did you play that new gotちゃcha game that just came out?
B：No, and how many times do I have to tell you that they're called GAちゃCHA games?

While many people today are familiar with the term "がちゃgacha" from video games, the word actually originated from the old capsule toy machines in Japan. We also have them in the states: you put in a quarter or two, turn the knob, and a toy pops out! Do you remember the sound the capsules made as you turned the knob? In Japan, they have an onomatopoeia for that sound: がちゃがちゃgacha gacha (clatter clatter), which is the noise the capsules make as they clatter about in the machine.

Nowadays, you mostly see the word used for videos games where you exchange currency to get characters and such randomly, just like a capsule toy machine.


Hiragana ちゅ
Romaji chu
Pronunciation choo

ちゅchu is pronounced like a combination of ちchi (minus the "i") and ゆyu (minus the "y"). The sound you create should be similar to choo ("ch" sound followed by "oo"). It's also pronounced like the choo in "choose" and "choo-choo."

I ask my son to ちゅchoose a toy and he said that he wanted the "ちゅchoo-ちゅchoo."

Now if you want to refer to choo-choo trains in Japanese, DON'T use ちゅchu as this is actually an onomatopoeia used for kissing (e.g., ちゅうchuu～; *kiss*). Instead, the word you'd want for choo-choo is しゅっしゅっぽっぽshusshuppoppo or しゅぽしゅぽshupo shupo which are words that mimic the sound that steam engines make.

If you're familiar with some Japanese media already, you may have heard of the word "pyon" which is an onomatopoeia used for jumping, especially for bunny rabbits. ぴょpyo is pronounced exactly like the pyo in pyon! Click here for an example.

Bunny rabbits go ぴょpyoんnぴょpyoんn!

みゃmya みゅmyu みょmyo

Next up is the みゃmya column which takes the "m" sound from みmi and combines it with ゃya, ゅyu, and ょyo.


Hiragana みゃ
Romaji mya
Pronunciation myah

みゃmya is pronounced like a combination of みmi (minus the "i") and やya. The sound you create should be similar to myah ("m" sound followed by "yah"). If you add an おo on the end, you get みゃおmyao which sounds very close to "meow."

In America, cats go みゃmyaおo, but in Japan they go にゃあnyaa.

Hiragana みゅ
Romaji myu
Pronunciation myoo

みゅmyu is pronounced like a combination of みmi (minus the "i") and ゆyu. The sound you create should be similar to myoo ("m" sound followed by "you"). It's pronounced just like "mew" which is the sound a crying kitten makes.

While walking home from school, Kan-tan heard みゅmewing in a nearby alley. When she checked, she found a little kitten crying alone in a box.

Romaji myo
Pronunciation myoh

りゃrya りゅryu りょryo

Last up is the りゃrya column which takes the "r" sound from りri and combines it with ゃya, ゅyu, and ょyo. If you remember from the らra column, the Japanese R is a cross between an R and an L which can make this column a little tricky. If you're having trouble with the pronunciation, feel free to go back and re-read our explanation in the らra column.


Hiragana りゃ
Romaji rya
Pronunciation r/l-yah

りゃrya is pronounced like a combination of りri (minus the "i") and やya. The sound you create should be similar to ryah (Japanese "r" sound followed by "yah").

I also have nothing for this one, so try punching the air and scream: HI-りゃRYAH　

Hiragana りゅ
Romaji ryu
Pronunciation r/l-yoo

りゅryu is pronounced like a combination of りri (minus the "i") and ゆyu. The sound you create should be similar to ryoo (Japanese "r" sound followed by "you"). It's pronounced a little like the lu in "lute", but of course with the combined r/l sound of the Japanese R and a slight "y" sound in the beginning like with ゆyu.

Bards used to go from town to town playing their りゅlute wherever they went.

In Japanese, lute is actually spelt りゅーとryuuto, but in Katakana.


Hiragana りょ
Romaji ryo
Pronunciation r/l-yoh

りょryo is pronounced like a combination of りri (minus the "i") and よyo. The sound you create should be similar to ryoh (Japanese "r" sound followed by "yoh"). If you've ever heard of "ryokan" (a traditional Japanese inn), it's prounced exactly like that beginning ryo.

If you ever visit Japan, we highly recommend staying at a りょryoかんkan!
